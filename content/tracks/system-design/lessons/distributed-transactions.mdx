## Intuition

At a wedding the officiant asks each partner "Do you?" and both say yes. Only then does the officiant declare them married. Once you have said yes you cannot walk away; you must wait for the declaration. If the officiant faints mid-ceremony, both stand stuck until someone revives them. That is **two-phase commit**.

Now plan a holiday: hotel, then car, then flight, with three companies. If the flight fails, nobody can roll back the hotel's database; you email to cancel, perhaps paying a fee. That is a **saga**.

**The problem.** An order must be recorded in the Orders database *and* paid in the Payments database. A normal transaction (isolation: see [Consistency models](/system-design/consistency-models/)) lives inside one database. With two, a crash between the commits leaves one done and one not. We want all or nothing.

**Two-phase commit (2PC).** A **coordinator** drives the **participants**.
1. **Prepare.** Each participant does the work, writes its changes durably, **keeps its locks** and votes YES (a promise that it can commit) or NO.
2. **Decide.** If all vote YES, the coordinator **durably logs COMMIT**. That log write is the **decision point**: the outcome is fixed even if everything crashes. Then it tells everyone to commit; any NO or missing vote means ABORT.

A participant that voted YES can no longer decide alone. The coordinator may already have logged COMMIT and told another participant, so aborting could split the outcome; it must **wait for the decision**, still holding its locks. So if the coordinator dies, prepared participants block, and other transactions queue behind their locks.

On restart, a coordinator with a logged decision re-sends it. With none, many implementations use **presumed abort**: no record means abort. That is a common convention, not a law of the protocol.

2PC solves atomic commit, not consensus, and blocks on coordinator failure; variants such as three-phase commit and Paxos Commit aim to reduce that.

**XA** is a standard interface for running 2PC across databases and brokers; support and performance vary by product. Microservices usually avoid it: **availability** (every participant must be up), **coupling** (each exposes a transactional resource) and **lock time** (locks span round trips and coordinator outages).

**Sagas.** Garcia-Molina and Salem (1987) split a long transaction into **local transactions** T1, T2, … each with a **compensating action** C1, C2, …. If a step fails, run the compensations of the completed steps in **reverse** order.
- A compensation is a **semantic undo**, not a rollback: the commit already happened and others may have seen it. "Cancel booking" may leave a record or a fee.
- Sagas give **atomicity (eventually), consistency and durability, but no isolation**: ACD without I.
- A compensation must be **safe to retry** and must **eventually succeed**, or be escalated to a human.
- The **pivot** is the go/no-go step. Steps before it are compensatable; steps after it are **retriable** and must eventually succeed. Put the hard-to-undo step as late as possible.

In **orchestration**, a central service tells each participant what to do next. In **choreography**, services react to each other's events: no central component, but a scattered flow.

Each step carries a **producer-assigned idempotency key**, and commands leave a service through the **outbox** from [Queues & streams](/system-design/queues-streams/), so a crash cannot lose them.

**The isolation problem.** Between steps, others see partial results, such as a hotel booked for a trip that later fails. Mitigate with a **pending** status (a **semantic lock**): other flows treat the record as provisional until the saga ends.

**TCC (try-confirm-cancel)** is a related pattern: each service offers a *try* that tentatively claims capacity, then a *confirm* or *cancel*. It builds in the pending state, but every service must implement all three idempotently and expire unconfirmed tries.

```text
 Car fails:   T1 ok, T2 fails → C1       saga ends "failed"
 After pivot: T3 ok, T4 fails → retry T4 until it succeeds (never undo T3)
```

## Example

A trip-booking saga with an orchestrator. Hotel and car have free cancellation and the flight ticket is non-refundable, so the ticket is the pivot and comes third. The repeatable itinerary email follows.

| Step | Action | Compensation |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | Book hotel | Cancel hotel |
| T2 | Book car | Cancel car |
| T3 | Issue ticket (pivot) | none: forward only |
| T4 | Send itinerary | none: retry until sent |

**When the car booking fails.** T1 has committed and T2 is refused. The orchestrator marks the saga `compensating`, runs C2 (a harmless no-op if the car was never booked, so a cancel of nothing must succeed) then C1, and ends in `failed`; no ticket was issued. If the car call *times out*, the outcome is unknown, which is why C2 runs anyway.

The sketch is generic, with placeholder helpers, and is not any framework.

```python
import time

class StepFailed(Exception): ...      # definite refusal
class TransientError(Exception): ...  # timeout or 5xx: outcome unknown

def retry(fn, attempts=8):
    for i in range(attempts):
        try:
            return fn()
        except TransientError:
            time.sleep(min(30, 2 ** i))
    raise StepFailed("retries exhausted; escalate to a human")

def run_saga(sid, before_pivot, pivot, after_pivot, store):
    """before_pivot: [(name, do, undo)]; pivot, after_pivot: (name, do)."""
    s = store.load(sid) or {"started": [], "done": [], "status": "running"}
    if s["status"] in ("failed", "completed"):
        return s
    key = lambda name, sfx="": f"{sid}:{name}{sfx}"  # one key per step

    if s["status"] == "running":
        try:
            for name, do, _ in before_pivot + [(*pivot, None)]:
                if name in s["done"]:
                    continue                     # resumed after a crash
                s["started"].append(name)
                store.save(sid, s)               # persist BEFORE the call
                retry(lambda: do(idempotency_key=key(name)))
                s["done"].append(name)
                store.save(sid, s)
        except StepFailed:
            s["status"] = "compensating"
            store.save(sid, s)

    if s["status"] == "compensating":
        undo = {n: u for n, _, u in before_pivot}
        for name in reversed(s["started"]):      # reverse order, failed step included
            if name in undo:
                retry(lambda: undo[name](idempotency_key=key(name, ":undo")))
        s["status"] = "failed"
        store.save(sid, s)
        return s

    for name, do in after_pivot:                 # retriable steps
        retry(lambda: do(idempotency_key=key(name)))
    s["status"] = "completed"
    store.save(sid, s)
    return s
```

State is saved before every call, so a restart resumes. Each step has one fixed key, so a retry is recognised, not applied twice. A real orchestrator would publish commands through an outbox.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating 2PC as free.** A coordinator outage freezes locks across services. Fix: use it among systems you control, with short transactions.
- **Assuming a saga is isolated.** Readers see steps that later compensate. Fix: a pending status or semantic lock.
- **Compensating as if it were rollback.** Others saw the effect. Fix: design each compensation as a business action that may leave a trace.
- **Non-idempotent or silently failing compensations.** Fix: idempotent undo, backoff, and a human alert after a limit.
- **Pivot too early.** A later failure cannot undo it. Fix: compensatable steps first, only retriable steps after the pivot.
- **A fresh idempotency key per retry.** The service applies it again. Fix: assign one key per step before the first attempt and persist it.

## Tips & tricks

- First ask whether data that must change together belongs in one service.
- Rule of thumb: 2PC for a few systems you control; a saga where services are independent and availability matters.
- Per step, write down its compensation, its key and its side of the pivot. Measure saga duration, stuck sagas, compensation failures and the oldest pending record.
- Interview phrasing: "A saga with an orchestrator: compensatable steps first, pivot late, retriable steps after. Idempotency key per step, outbox for messages, a pending status, because sagas give no isolation."
