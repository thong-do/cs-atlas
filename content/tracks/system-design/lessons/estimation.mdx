## Intuition

Before you pick a database, a cache or a number of servers, you need a sense of size. Is it 50 requests a second or 50,000? Is it 10 GB or 10 PB? **Back-of-the-envelope estimation** answers these questions in a few minutes, with arithmetic small enough for the back of an envelope.

The answers decide the shape of the design early:

- **Does it fit on one machine?** A typical server holds hundreds of GB of RAM and tens of TB of SSD (the largest hold several TB of RAM). If the data fits, keep it simple. If it doesn't, you need [replication & sharding](/system-design/replication-sharding/).
- **Does the hot data fit in RAM?** If it does, a [cache](/system-design/caching/) can serve most reads from memory.
- **Is the outbound traffic heavy, or far from users?** Gigabits per second of images or video belong on a [CDN](/system-design/dns-cdn/), not on your own servers.
- **Can one database primary keep up with the writes?** If it can't, you partition.

**The goal is the right order of magnitude, not the right digits.** Your inputs are guesses, such as daily users, actions per user and average object size, and each one can easily be off by 2×. Working the arithmetic out to four digits on top of that is false precision. What matters is which side of a limit you land on. 30 GB and 60 GB both fit in one server's memory, and 6 TB does not fit in a typical one. An estimate within 2× of the truth rarely moves you across such a line, but one that is 10× off can.

Estimation is a loop. State your assumptions, multiply, round, compare the result with a limit, and adjust.

## Visual

A reference ladder of numbers worth remembering. Each one is a rough order of magnitude.

**Powers of two vs powers of ten.** Each step of 2¹⁰ is close to a step of 10³.

| Power of two | Exact value | About | Prefix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2¹⁰ | 1,024 | 10³, a thousand | KB |
| 2²⁰ | 1,048,576 | 10⁶, a million | MB |
| 2³⁰ | 1,073,741,824 | 10⁹, a billion | GB |
| 2⁴⁰ | 1,099,511,627,776 | 10¹², a trillion | TB |

On this site KB = 1,000 bytes; the 2.4% difference from 1,024 never matters at this precision. The gap grows to about 10% at TB, which still doesn't matter.

**Latency.** Rounded orders of magnitude. Real hardware and networks vary by 2× or more.

| Operation | About | Compared with L1 |
|---|---|---|
| L1 cache reference | 1 ns | 1× |
| Main memory reference | 100 ns | 100× |
| SSD random read | 100 µs (tens of µs on fast NVMe) | 100,000× |
| Round trip within a datacenter | 0.5 ms | 500,000× |
| Intercontinental round trip (e.g. US west coast to Europe) | 100–150 ms | about 10⁸× |

Light in fibre covers about 200 km per ms. A round trip to a server 10,000 km away covers 20,000 km of fibre, so it takes at least 100 ms, and no server upgrade can beat that.

**Time.**

| Period | Seconds | About |
|---|---|---|
| 1 hour | 3,600 | 3.6 × 10³ |
| 1 day | 86,400 | 10⁵ |
| 1 month (30 days) | 2,592,000 | 2.6 × 10⁶, or 2.6 M |
| 1 year (365 days) | 31,536,000 | 3.15 × 10⁷ |

Two handy consequences: 1 M requests a day is about 12 per second (1,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11.6), and a year is close to π × 10⁷ seconds.

**Bits and bytes.** Networks are rated in bits per second, and storage is measured in bytes. 1 byte = 8 bits, so 1 Gbit/s = 125 MB/s.

## Example

Estimate the load for a photo-sharing app. These numbers are illustrative assumptions:

- 45 M daily active users (DAU).
- 1 in 3 of them uploads one photo a day.
- Each user views 30 photos a day, and each view serves the whole photo.
- A stored photo averages 300 KB. Its metadata row (owner, caption, timestamps) is about 1 KB.
- The busiest hour runs at **3× the daily average**. This is the peak factor; measure it on real traffic when you can.
- The photo store keeps 3 replicas.

| Quantity | Arithmetic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Uploads per day | 45 M × 1/3 | 15 M |
| Average upload rate | 15,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s | about 174/s |
| Peak upload rate | 174/s × 3 | about 520/s |
| Views per day | 45 M × 30 | 1.35 B |
| Average view rate | 1,350,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s | 15,625/s |
| Peak view rate | 15,625/s × 3 | 46,875/s, about 47,000/s |
| Photo storage per day | 15 M × 300 KB | 4.5 TB |
| Metadata per day | 15 M × 1 KB | 15 GB, 0.3% of the photo bytes |
| Photo storage per year | 4.5 TB × 365 | 1,642.5 TB, about 1.6 PB |
| With 3 replicas | 1,642.5 TB × 3 | 4,927.5 TB, about 4.9 PB |
| Average egress | 15,625/s × 300 KB | 4.6875 GB/s, about 4.7 GB/s |
| Average egress in bits | 4.6875 GB/s × 8 | 37.5 Gbit/s |
| Peak egress | 37.5 Gbit/s × 3 | 112.5 Gbit/s |

The 10⁵ shortcut for a day gives the same picture: 15 M ÷ 10⁵ = 150 uploads/s and 1.35 B ÷ 10⁵ = 13,500 views/s, both well within 2×.

What the numbers say:

- **Uploads are easy.** About 520 writes/s at peak is modest; a few app servers and one database primary can handle it.
- **Metadata is small; photos are not.** 15 GB a day of rows is about 5.5 TB a year (15 GB × 365 = 5,475 GB), which one database can hold for a while. Photos take petabytes, so they go in an object store, not on database disks.
- **Egress needs a CDN.** One 10 Gbit/s network card moves 1.25 GB/s. An average of 37.5 Gbit/s would keep almost four such cards fully busy all day, and the 112.5 Gbit/s peak would need more than eleven. A CDN serves popular photos from its edges and keeps most of those bytes off your servers.
- **Reads dominate.** 1.35 B views against 15 M uploads is 90 reads per write, so design the read path first.

## Pitfalls

- **Average vs peak.** Traffic is not flat. A system sized for the daily average falls over in the busiest hour. Always state a peak factor and size for the peak.
- **Bits vs bytes.** Link speeds are in bits, and file sizes are in bytes. Forgetting the ×8 makes a transfer look 8 times faster than it is. Write `Gbit/s` or `GB/s`, never a bare `G`.
- **Forgetting replication.** Storage estimates usually count one copy. Three replicas triple the disk, and backups and indexes add more.
- **Counting only half of the data.** Count the metadata but not the payload, or the payload but not the metadata, and you miss whichever part is large. In the Example, counting only the 1 KB rows would make storage look 300 times smaller than it is. For tiny records the reverse holds, and indexes and per-row overhead can rival the data itself. List every kind of data you store, then add up its size.
- **Per-day vs per-second slips.** Dividing a daily count by 3,600 (seconds in an hour) or 1,440 (minutes in a day) instead of 86,400 makes the rate 24 or 60 times too high. Comparing a per-day count with a per-second limit is off by 86,400. Convert everything to per second before comparing.

## Tips & tricks

- **Round to one significant figure early.** 86,400 becomes 10⁵, 174 becomes 200, and 46,875 becomes 50,000. The arithmetic gets easy, and the result usually stays within 2×; if several roundings push the same way, round one of them the other way.
- **Work in powers of ten.** 15 M × 300 KB is 1.5 × 10⁷ × 3 × 10⁵ B = 4.5 × 10¹² B = 4.5 TB. Multiply the small numbers and add the exponents.
- **Write units on every line.** `views/s × KB/view = KB/s`. If the units don't cancel to what you want, the formula is wrong.
- **Sanity-check against something known.** Compare the result with one machine's limits, such as hundreds of GB of RAM, tens of TB of disk and a 10 Gbit/s network card. Or compare it with a system you know. If a small app comes out bigger than a famous one, recheck your assumptions.
- In interviews, say your assumptions out loud, round boldly, and say which design choice each number drives.
