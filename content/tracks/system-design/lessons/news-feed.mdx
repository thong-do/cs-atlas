## Intuition

Picture a newspaper stand that makes a personal front page for every reader. Assemble it when the reader walks up, and it is slow for people who follow many sources. Slot every article into every subscriber's page as it is printed, and it is costly for an author with millions of subscribers. A **news feed** is that stand: users follow accounts, and opening the app shows their recent posts.

It is interesting because reads and writes pull in opposite directions, and one system must serve users with 50 followers and with 50 million. The core question is where to spend work: **fan-out on write** (push) or **fan-out on read** (pull)?

## Requirements & estimates

**Functional.** Publish, edit and delete posts. Follow and unfollow. Read a paged feed of recent posts from followed accounts.

**Non-functional (assumptions).** Feed reads are fast (p99 under a few hundred ms). Eventual consistency is fine: a new post may take seconds to appear, but a deleted one should vanish promptly.

**Estimates.** These numbers are illustrative. See [Back-of-the-envelope estimation](/system-design/estimation/) for the method. Assume 100 M daily active users (DAU), 10% of them post once a day, each user follows about 200 accounts, and each reads their feed 10 times a day.

| Quantity | Arithmetic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Posts per day | 100 M × 10% | 10 M |
| Write QPS | 10 M ÷ 86,400 | about 116/s; about 350/s at a 3× peak |
| Followers per author | see note below | about 200 on average |
| Fan-out writes per day | 10 M posts × 200 followers | 2 × 10^9 |
| Fan-out writes/s | 2 × 10^9 ÷ 86,400 | about 23,000/s; about 70,000/s at 3× |
| Feed reads per day | 100 M × 10 | 1 × 10^9 |
| Read QPS | 10^9 ÷ 86,400 | about 11,600/s; about 35,000/s at 3× |
| Feed cache | 100 M users × 500 ids × 8 B | about 400 GB of raw ids; scores and overhead add several times that |
| Post storage | 10 M × 1 KB (text and metadata) | about 10 GB/day, 3.7 TB/year |

*Follows versus followers.* The 200 is an average of **follows** per user, but fan-out cost depends on **followers** per author. In a closed graph where everyone is counted, each follow edge adds one to a follower count and one to a follows count, so the two averages are equal. Heavy posters may differ, so treat 200 as rough.

Push turns 116 post writes/s into 23,000 feed writes/s, about 200 times more. Pull inverts that: cheap writes, but each read gathers posts from about 200 accounts.

## Design walkthrough

**API.**

- `POST /v1/posts` with `{text}` returns `201` and `{postId}`.
- `GET /v1/feed?limit=20&cursor=...` returns posts and a `nextCursor`.
- `PUT` and `DELETE /v1/follows/{userId}`; `PATCH` and `DELETE /v1/posts/{postId}`.

**Data model.** `posts(post_id, author_id, text, created_at, deleted)`. `follows(follower_id, followee_id)`, readable in both directions (who do I follow, who follows me). A feed entry in the cache is a sorted list per user of `(post_id, score)`, where the score is a timestamp or a rank. Use time-sortable post ids. Shard the post store by `post_id` to spread load evenly, or by `author_id` (see [replication & sharding](/system-design/replication-sharding/)). Author sharding makes timelines a single-shard read but risks a hot shard for a popular author; id sharding spreads load but makes a timeline read a scatter-gather.

**Push (fan-out on write).** When a post is created, a worker looks up the author's followers and inserts the post id into each follower's cached feed. Reading is one lookup. The price is write amplification: about 23,000 feed writes/s.

**Pull (fan-out on read).** Fetch recent posts from each followed account and merge them. Nothing is stored per follower, but a read touches about 200 timelines, so latency grows with the follow count.

**Hybrid.** Push for ordinary authors and pull for celebrities. Choose a follower threshold, for example 100,000 (an illustrative figure to tune against measured fan-out cost). Authors above it are not fanned out; a reader merges their timeline with the precomputed feed, as the animation shows. Large systems are often described as mixing the two, but details vary, so choose by your own numbers.

**Feed cache.** Store **post ids only**, not post bodies. Ids are small (8 B), so 500 per user is affordable, and an edit does not touch thousands of feeds. At read time, **hydrate**: batch-fetch the posts by id from a [cache](/system-design/caching/) backed by the post store. Popular posts are read by many users, so the hit ratio is high.

**Fan-out through a queue.** The post service stores the post, publishes an event to a queue ([queues & streams](/system-design/queues-streams/)) and returns. Delivery is at-least-once, so workers must be idempotent: the post id, assigned by the post service (the producer), is the idempotency key, so inserting it into a feed twice leaves one entry. Partition the stream by author, with one consumer group for the fan-out workers. Split a big follower list into chunks so one slow chunk retries alone.

**Ordering and ranking.** The simplest feed is chronological: sort by post id or time. A ranked feed scores a few hundred merged candidates (recency, affinity, predicted engagement) and returns the top. It adds read-path CPU, so measure first.

**Pagination with cursors.** Return an opaque cursor encoding the last item seen; the next request asks for items strictly after it. An offset (`?page=3`) breaks when the feed changes: ten new posts shift old items down, so page 2 repeats ten of them, and deletes make the reader skip some (see [client-server & HTTP](/system-design/client-server-http/)). A cursor is anchored to an item, not a position, and the store can seek straight to it. For a chronological feed, `(time, post_id)` works. For a ranked feed, a `(score, post_id)` cursor on a live rank can repeat or skip items as scores change, so page over a snapshot of the ranked candidate list, keyed by a session id.

**Deletes, edits and unfollows.** Because feeds hold ids, an edit only changes the post record, and readers see it on hydration. For a delete, flag the post and **filter at read time**, and invalidate the post's cache entry ([caching](/system-design/caching/)). Hydration drops flagged posts, and the reader fetches a few extra ids to fill the page. A background job can purge ids later. Do the same for unfollow: instead of rewriting a feed, filter entries whose author is no longer followed when you build the page. The cost is an occasional short page.

**Inactive users.** Fan-out to people who have not opened the app in weeks is wasted. Skip them, and on login rebuild the feed with a pull, then resume pushing. The cost is a slower first load.

**Failure handling.** If the feed cache loses data, rebuild feeds with the pull path: a cold cache costs latency, not correctness. If workers fall behind, feeds are stale but readable. Replicate the post store.

## Pitfalls

- **Pushing for everyone.** One celebrity post becomes millions of writes. Add a threshold and pull for those authors.
- **Pulling for everyone.** Each read fans in across hundreds of timelines. Precompute feeds for ordinary authors.
- **Storing post bodies in feeds.** Edits and deletes then need millions of updates. Store ids and hydrate.
- **Offset pagination.** New posts shift pages, causing duplicates and gaps. Use cursors.
- **Non-idempotent workers.** At-least-once delivery then duplicates feed entries. Make the insert idempotent.
- **Fanning out to dormant accounts.** The writes are never read. Skip inactive users.

## Tips & tricks

- **Structure the answer.** Numbers, API and data model, push versus pull, the hybrid threshold, cache and hydration, then pagination, deletes and failures.
- **Say the trade-offs out loud.** Push buys fast reads with write amplification, pull buys cheap writes with slow reads, and hybrid pays complexity for the skew.
- **Show the arithmetic.** 116 posts/s becoming about 23,000 feed writes/s makes fan-out the central problem.
- **Commit to one design:** hybrid, ids in the cache, idempotent queue workers, cursors, lazy filtering.
- **Measure** fan-out lag, cache hit ratio, hydration and feed read p99, and queue depth.
